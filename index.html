The Complete Guide to Renovating a Period Home Without Losing Its Soul

By Elena Marsh & James Okonkwo 22 min read

Renovating a period property is an exercise in restraint. The temptation is to strip everything back to bare brick, open up every wall, and install a wall of glass. But the most successful renovations are those that listen to the building first. They understand its original logic — the way it was planned, the materials it was built from, the way light moves through it — and then they intervene with a light touch.

1. Start with the plan, not the finishes

Before you choose a single tile or tap, you need to understand the existing floor plan. Period homes often have a clear hierarchy of spaces: formal rooms at the front, service rooms at the back. The mistake many renovators make is to erase that hierarchy entirely. Instead, consider keeping the front rooms as quieter, more formal spaces, while opening up the rear for kitchen, dining, and family life. This preserves the building’s character while giving you the modern flow you need.

2. Respect the original materials

Original floorboards, cornices, ceiling roses, and fireplaces are not obstacles — they are assets. Restoring them, rather than replacing them, almost always looks better and costs less than you think. If floorboards are too far gone, consider reclaimed timber that matches the original species and width. If cornices are damaged, a specialist can take a mould and reproduce them exactly. These details are what make a period home feel authentic.

What to keep, what to replace

  • Keep: original staircases, internal doors, window shutters, and any decorative plasterwork.
  • Replace with care: modern double glazing that mimics the original window profile; new floorboards only where the originals are unsalvageable.
  • Remove only if necessary: non‑original partitions from the 1970s or 80s that block light and flow.

3. Insulate, but don’t suffocate

Period homes are notoriously drafty. But sealing them up completely can cause damp and poor air quality. The solution is a breathable approach: use natural insulation (wood fibre, sheep’s wool, or hemp) that allows moisture to move through the building fabric. Avoid cement‑based renders and gypsum plasters on solid walls; they trap moisture and cause long‑term damage. Lime plaster and breathable paints are your friends.

4. Lighting: layer for atmosphere

Period homes often have high ceilings and large windows — perfect for layered lighting. Avoid the temptation to install a single row of downlights. Instead, use a mix of pendant lights, wall sconces, and floor lamps. Dimmable warm LEDs (2700K) will mimic the soft glow of candlelight that these rooms were originally designed for.

5. Add modern interventions with confidence

You don’t have to make everything look old. A crisp, modern kitchen or a minimalist bathroom can sit beautifully within a period shell, as long as the proportions and materials are considered. The key is contrast: old and new should be clearly different, not a muddy imitation. Think of a sleek steel and glass extension attached to a stone cottage — the contrast makes both parts sing.

Ultimately, renovating a period home is about stewardship. You are one in a long line of people who will live there. The best renovations are those that leave the building stronger, more beautiful, and ready for the next hundred years.

About Gridline Home Ideas

Gridline Home Ideas is an independent editorial publication dedicated to the art and craft of interior design and architecture. We believe that good design is not about following trends, but about understanding how people live.

Our writers and editors are practising designers, architects, and journalists who care deeply about the details — from the way light falls across a room to the precise specification of a door handle.

We publish in‑depth guides, project tours, and practical advice. No fluff, no filler. Just useful knowledge for anyone who wants to make a better home.
